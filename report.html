Functional Hypernetworks: Single-Pass Weight Generation
with Provably Better Initialisations

DJ Swanevelder

Department of Applied Mathematics, Stellenbosch University — March 2026

Abstract

We train hypernetworks that generate complete neural network weights from prototype images in a single forward pass. A direct MLP generator (50K target parameters) achieves 84.6% zero-shot accuracy on unseen EMNIST classes and reaches a statistically significant better minimum than Kaiming initialisation after fine-tuning (+0.53pp, p<10-8). To scale beyond the 50K barrier, we introduce a SANE-based tokenised architecture (109K parameters) combining a functional Transformer autoencoder with a latent-space hypernetwork, achieving 81.4% zero-shot on fully unseen tasks with a persistent better-minimum effect (p=0.00006). Functional loss is the critical enabler: replacing it with MSE collapses reconstruction accuracy from 97% to 33% and eliminates the better-minimum effect entirely.

3 Method

3.1 Problem Setup

Each task is a 3-way classification over EMNIST characters (62 total classes). The target network is a small CNN or MLP classifier. Tasks are conditioned on prototype embeddings: 20 example images per class, averaged through a learned encoder. Classes 0–49 are seen during meta-training; classes 50–61 are held out entirely. At evaluation, we sample tasks with varying novelty (0/3 to 3/3 unseen classes) and measure both zero-shot accuracy and accuracy after k fine-tuning steps with SGD on fresh support data.

Figure 2. SANE pipeline. Top: Transformer autoencoder encodes per-neuron tokens into a latent z and reconstructs weights. Bottom: At generation time, a latent hypernetwork maps prototypes to z, then the frozen decoder produces weights.

Direct generation fails at 109K (stuck at chance). SANE decomposes the weight vector into per-neuron tokens, each containing the incoming/outgoing weights and bias of a single neuron. A Transformer autoencoder learns a compressed latent space over these token sequences using functional reconstruction loss (classifying with the reconstructed weights). A separate latent-space hypernetwork then maps prototype embeddings to this latent space, and the frozen decoder produces the full weight vector.

3.4 Training

3-Stage Pipeline
Stage 1Functional AE: train encoder+decoder on weight reconstruction via classification loss, 500 epochs, early stopping
Stage 2Latent hypernetwork: train MLP to predict z from prototypes, AE frozen, 200 epochs
Stage 3End-to-end: unfreeze all, fine-tune jointly with functional loss, 100 epochs, lr=1e-5
Key Hyperparameters
OptimiserAdam, lr=3e-4 (stages 1–2), 1e-5 (stage 3)
Functional lossCE on eval split, clamped at ln(3) to prevent divergence
Batch size64 tasks per batch
AE latent dim256
Transformer4 layers, 4 heads, dim=128

Table 1. 50K direct generation on all-unseen (3/3) tasks. p-values from paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test vs Kaiming+SGD at 3000 steps. Win counts over 220 evaluation tasks.

The generated initialisation provides an enormous head start: 84.6% zero-shot vs 33% chance, and after only 10 fine-tuning steps it reaches 92.2% while random initialisation sits at 59.8%. At convergence (3000 steps), the generated initialisation still maintains a statistically significant advantage of +0.53pp over Kaiming (p<10-8), winning 174 out of 220 tasks. This confirms a genuine better-minimum effect, not merely faster convergence. With Adam, the gap narrows but remains significant (+0.18pp, p=0.032).

The generated initialisation dominates across the entire fine-tuning trajectory. This makes the approach especially valuable in compute-constrained or few-shot deployment scenarios where only a handful of gradient steps are available.

The SANE approach produces a consistent better-minimum effect across all novelty levels. Remarkably, the effect is strongest on fully unseen tasks (3/3 unseen: +0.41pp, 38/50 wins, p=0.00006), suggesting the model has learned generalisable structural priors about how classifiers should be initialised rather than memorising task-specific solutions. The 2/3 condition at p=0.056 is marginally significant, likely due to higher variance when two of three classes are novel.

4.4 Ablation: Functional vs MSE Autoencoder

AE Loss Recon Acc Zero-shot (3/3) Gap vs Kaiming p-value Better Minimum?
MSE-only 33% 69% −0.46pp — No
Functional 97% 81% +0.41pp 0.00006 Yes
Table 4. Ablation on AE training loss for SANE (109K). Recon acc = classification accuracy of the reconstructed weight vector. MSE achieves non-trivial zero-shot generation but the autoencoder's latent space does not preserve functional structure, so the better-minimum effect vanishes.

This is the most informative ablation. The MSE-only autoencoder learns a latent space that captures some weight structure (69% zero-shot is well above chance), but the reconstructed weights only classify at 33% -- the autoencoder has learned correlations in weight space that do not correspond to functional behaviour. The functional autoencoder, by contrast, reconstructs networks that actually work (97% accuracy), and this functional fidelity is what enables the latent hypernetwork to produce initialisations that converge to better minima.

5 Discussion

  • What works: Functional loss is the single most important design decision. It enables both high-fidelity weight reconstruction (97% vs 33% for MSE) and the better-minimum effect. The combination of prototype conditioning and functional training produces initialisations that are not just closer to good solutions but land in better basins of the loss landscape, as evidenced by the persistent advantage even at convergence with SGD and Adam.
  • Limitation: The better-minimum effect, while statistically robust, is small in absolute terms at convergence (+0.41pp for SANE, +0.53pp for direct). The primary practical value is in the low-step regime where the advantage is enormous (32pp+ after 10 steps). Scaling to larger target networks remains untested -- the SANE tokenisation approach is promising but the current experiments are limited to 109K parameters.
  • Future work: Three directions: (1) scaling SANE to larger architectures (ResNets, ViTs) by exploiting the per-neuron tokenisation's natural modularity; (2) investigating whether the better-minimum effect compounds across tasks in continual learning settings; (3) replacing the prototype conditioning with task-description embeddings to enable text-conditioned weight generation.
